## Letter transcription

> Dear James,
>
> You will have seen the National Data Guardian's letter dated 9th October, published on the NDG website today, relating to the Federated Data Platform (FDP), and Palantir's role as a supplier. The NDG made a number of claims in that letter which I would like to respond to.
>
> I begin by noting that in November 2023, the NDG observed that: "If a programme does not have a strong enough voice in the public discourse and there is a lack of trust, it creates a knowledge gap. And in this gap, alternative narratives can take hold, becoming a breeding ground for speculation, misinformation and discontent."
>
> That was right. False information can all too easily creep into public discourse around government programmes, especially in health. All of us, whatever role we hold, have a responsibility to dispel such claims. Regrettably, this is not addressed in her most recent letter and its annex.
>
> The advice that the NDG offered you at the conclusion of the main body of her letter is sensible. "Public and professional trust should be given substantial weight when evaluating any supplier of a system involving people's health and care data." I agree. "Decisions about the future of the FDP should be supported by clear, objective evidence of the platform's benefits and limitations, communicated transparently." I agree. "Concerns about interoperability, vendor lock-in and data sovereignty must be addressed." Again, I agree.
>
> However, there are a number of issues with the considerations, and underlying evidence base, that the NDG has presented in her letter. It offers no scrutiny of how ideologically motivated campaign groups are eroding trust by sharing false information. It ignores critical facts, including that opt-outs have risen by 0.3 per cent in nearly three years since Palantir was awarded the contract - less than it rose in a single week during COVID. And it draws sweeping conclusions that are not supported by the scant evidence it relies on.
>
> There is a serious debate to be had about how we protect privacy and security as technology plays a greater role in patient care. Sadly the National Data Guardian appears to have been captured by campaign groups that have zero interest in building trust and every interest in stoking fear, and, as a result, this intervention fails to contribute to it.
>
> Below, I set out three areas where we believe the NDG's intervention falls short. We would welcome the opportunity to work with the NDG and your Department on a fuller, more objective assessment of public and professional sentiment about the FDP.
>
> **Maintaining trust and preventing the spread of false information**
>
> The NDG's letter rightly stresses the importance of public and professional trust in the FDP, built through accuracy, transparency and consistently high standards.
>
> However, these standards must also be applied to campaign groups. Regrettably, the NDG does not assess their behaviour. It only notes their role in attracting "significant participation" in the debate, without reviewing the tactics and false information they have used to generate it.
>
> For example, the politically motivated Good Law Project describes its team, in its own published account, taking "fake NHS flyers" into a hospital. The material and campaign video carried HM Government and NHS branding, despite the Department of Health and Social Care's request for the latter to be removed, presented itself as a "Health alert" and announced "An infection in the NHS", identifying that infection as Palantir.
>
> This activity steals the authority of government health information to deliver false information in a place where people seek care. Sick and vulnerable patients should not have to work out whether a government-branded health alert in their hospital is a campaign stunt.
>
> We know the NDG is aware of the damaging impact this type of activity can have because it wrote about the impact of NHS branding on public trust in its report, Can communications create reasonable expectations about uses of health and care data?
>
> **Subjective, self-fulfilling arguments**
>
> The NDG states that some medical professionals and members of the public oppose Palantir and the FDP because of concerns about values. The letter goes on to say that assurances about "technical, legal and contractual safeguards" do not address these concerns.
>
> To be clear, Palantir's values are that technology can help transform public services and save lives. The NHS shares these values and our software helps it deliver them.
>
> But beyond that, is the NDG really saying that the data and facts don't matter here, and what a subset of people think or feel is more important? That would create a dangerously circular argument, in which campaign groups can whip up vocal but narrow opposition, then point to it and claim it represents evidence of broad public concern.
>
> Groups like the Good Law Project specialise in this. They claim to have generated over 83,000 letters on the FDP. To be clear, these are pro forma letters where you enter your name and postcode and the letter is generated. When the NDG talks of "significant volumes of correspondence", there is no acknowledgement of this practice, which is very likely driving much of it. More broadly, if we let this kind of activity dictate procurement practices or review decisions, it will harm public service delivery and patient care.
>
> **Limited evidence of public or professional concern**
>
> *Public*
>
> The NDG cites three main evidence bases for its assessment of public sentiment on Palantir and the FDP: media coverage, a single poll and national data opt-out rates.
>
> Media coverage volume or sentiment is not an accurate measure of public opinion. The NDG's letter cites a study by UPD. Here is a finding from that study: The Register and Byline Times typically originate Palantir stories, now joined by Foxglove and Amnesty, before being picked up by the Guardian, the Daily Mail and the Financial Times, where they reach a far larger audience. This does not constitute serious evidence of what the public actually think.
>
> The poll cited was commissioned by Eko. Eko is a campaign group that is currently hosting a petition to "suspend all Palantir contracts" because "Trump's digital Trojan horse is hoovering up our data - including your private patient data". Aside from this being a prime example of the kind of false information that the NDG should be concerned with, it is a clear indicator that this research should not be relied upon as impartial.
>
> Opt-outs in response to the care.data programme reportedly reached 1.2 million. This compares with the c.60,000 over the past four months you identified in your response to the Health and Social Care Select Committee. The opt-out rate since Palantir won the contract in November 2023 has risen by 0.3 percentage points to 5.7 per cent. None of this critical context is included in the letter.
>
> *Professional*
>
> The claims made by the NDG about professional concern are also not supported by evidence.
>
> The NDG references that "more than 100 NHS data and digital professionals" signed a letter objecting to continuation of the contract. According to the NHS's own data, there are 18,820 staff in the wider Digital, Data and Technology field, meaning the signatories amount to around 0.6 per cent of even this community. Many have made public statements about the efficacy of the software. This contrasts with the anonymity of the letter in question.
>
> The NDG also claims that there is evidence that "a significant number of local organisations" are reluctant to actively adopt FDP. Yet no evidence is provided to support this claim. You'll be aware that 170 NHS trusts and 35 ICBs have signed up to the Federated Data Platform. 142 trusts are live, and 137 trusts are actively reporting benefits.
>
> Yours sincerely,
